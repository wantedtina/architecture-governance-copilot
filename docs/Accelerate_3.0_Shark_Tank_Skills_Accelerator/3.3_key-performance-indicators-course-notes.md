# Key Performance Indicators: Developing, Implementing, and Using Winning KPIs

## Book Summary

**Book:** *Key Performance Indicators: Developing, Implementing, and
Using Winning KPIs*, 3rd Edition\
**Author:** David Parmenter

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Synopsis

Have you wondered why your organization is not performing at its
potential? By identifying performance measures and aligning them with
your organization's daily activities, you have a greater chance of
organizational success.

*Key Performance Indicators* (John Wiley & Sons, © 2015) shows you how
to achieve success by aligning your organization's daily activities with
its strategic objectives using key performance indicators.

Whether you're embarking on a new or retooled KPI initiative, this book
outlines how to develop and use KPIs for greater organizational success.

A successful KPI strategy involves these steps:

1.  Ensure organizational buy-in.
2.  Use in-house resources.
3.  Lead and sell the change.
4.  Identify your organization's critical success factors.
5.  Continually re-evaluate KPI measures.
6.  Make sure your KPIs drive performance.

## Understand Why You Need KPIs

Effective performance measurement can make the difference between a good
organization and a great one. By aligning and linking daily actions of
your organization to its critical success factors, you not only improve
performance, but you also create wider ownership, empower your people,
and fulfill your business objectives.

By developing and putting into practice an effective key performance
indicator (KPI) program, you will keep your business on course and help
it succeed.

### Why Measure Performance?

Measuring performance vitalizes performance. Through a strong
performance measurement program emphasizing key performance indicators,
you can motivate action, help people see progress, and bring into focus
what matters.

Because most people prefer being measured to being judged, measurement
increases employee morale. Measurement can also help with performance
consistency once expectations are clear. Because measurement requires
feedback, it offers an ongoing opportunity for a reality check.

### Make Sure KPI Measures Drive Performance

A reporting framework should operate at all levels of the organization
to ensure your KPIs drive performance.

Performance measures should help your organization align daily
activities to the organization's strategic objectives. This book has
been written to assist organizations with developing, implementing, and
using winning key performance indicators (KPIs)---those performance
measures that will make a profound difference.

## Know Your Measurement Types

Not all performance measures are KPIs. There are two measurement types:
**result indicators** and **performance indicators**. Within each
category, there are **key result indicators** and **key performance
indicators**.

### Result Indicators (RIs)

Result indicators summarize the activities of teams and give an overview
of how teams are working together.

Key result indicators (KRIs) provide a more comprehensive summary of
organizational performance in terms of activities. An example of an RI
or KRI might be a financial outcome. It must be measured quantitatively
rather than qualitatively. While RIs and KRIs reflect specific results,
they don't suggest corrective actions that might help you improve those
results.

### Performance Indicators (PIs)

Performance indicators delineate between teams and show which teams are
delivering---and how much.

Key performance indicators (KPIs) differentiate even more; they show how
an organization is performing according to its critical, or key, success
factors.

Through monitoring of both KRIs and KPIs, management can dramatically
increase performance. However, the author cautions that many
organizations inappropriately mix these four types, and stresses the
importance of understanding each one.

### Example: KPI vs. Critical Success Factor

A key result indicator might be net profit before tax.

A key performance indicator might measure performance against a critical
success factor (CSF), such as customer service complaints.

For example, British Airways identified one critical success factor as
the timely arrival and departure of airplanes. They defined it as a CSF
because this one factor has a ripple effect: if planes are delayed, they
incur extra costs in many areas, such as airport surcharges for lateness
and having to put up passengers overnight. Late planes also increase
customer and employee dissatisfaction.

The KPIs measure various aspects of performance that affect whether this
critical success factor is achieved. In this example, all KPIs would
have to do with the lateness of planes---for instance, are planes
arriving on time each day? Is it clear what corrective actions must be
taken, and by whom, if they are not?

## Key Characteristics of KPIs

Based on extensive analysis and discussions with workshop participants
over the years, with representatives from both the profit and nonprofit
sectors, the author has identified seven primary characteristics of
KPIs:

-   They are nonfinancial.
-   They are monitored with great frequency---from 24/7 to weekly at
    most.
-   They have the CEO's constant attention.
-   They are simple; they indicate exactly what needs to occur.
-   They have team-based responsibility; they are specific and
    action-oriented enough to be easily implemented.
-   They have a significant impact across many areas.
-   They have a limited dark side---in other words, they are unlikely to
    have unintended consequences.

### Example: Customer Complaints

An example of a KPI that fits these criteria might have to do with
complaints from key customers:

-   How often are they occurring?
-   Are they being tracked on a daily basis?
-   The KPI would not have a financial component, but would point toward
    recommendations on how these complaints should be handled across the
    team, with specific action steps to take immediately.
-   All team members would know their role in addressing the complaints
    so the issues are not recurring.
-   The KPIs would readily suggest what needs to happen in the present
    or future to reduce the complaints.

## Lay the Foundation for KPI Success

In order for your KPI process to thrive, the author recommends that you
perform the following foundation activities:

-   Facilitate partnership among staff, unions (if applicable), and
    other third parties.
-   Transfer power to the front line.
-   Report and measure what matters only.
-   Drive KPIs only using critical success factors.
-   Abandon processes that don't deliver.
-   Appoint a Chief Measurement Officer from within the organization.
-   Achieve full organizational buy-in of a winning KPI definition---in
    other words, make sure everyone is on board and understands each
    KPI.

As parts of a whole, each of these criteria helps lay the groundwork for
your KPI program to succeed. Make sure to understand each one and how it
works with the others.

## Develop and Execute Your KPI Plan

The author recommends following a six-stage process to implement your
organization's KPI plan. He believes more harm than good is created if
you try to identify your KPIs without going through these steps and
laying the groundwork. The author uses the analogy of preparing a house
before you paint it: you would never begin painting without first
washing the exterior, fixing the cracks, and sanding out the rough
spots.

### Six Steps to KPI Success

1.  **Ensure the CEO and senior management are committed to a KPI
    initiative.**
2.  **Train in-house staff to work full-time on the KPI project.** Don't
    hire outside consultants.
3.  **Lead and sell the change from within**, or your plan will not
    succeed. It must have buy-in by all involved, especially leadership.
4.  **Identify your organization's critical success factors (CSFs).**
    These are defined as operational issues or tasks engaged in on an
    ongoing or even routine basis by the staff.
5.  **Design appropriate measures for implementing related KPIs.**
6.  **Make sure those measures drive performance** by developing a
    reporting framework that cuts across all levels of the organization.

By following these steps, you will have a much greater chance of
creating and implementing a KPI plan that works for your company---and
supports your organizational mission.

## Empower Your Staff

Teams should be given responsibility to make more decisions and to
develop their own performance measures that support the organizational
KPIs. Employees should be empowered to take immediate action to address
situations that have a negative impact on KPIs.

To use the airline example, this might mean a staff member is authorized
to double-up cleaning staff on airplanes so planes are cleaned more
quickly if the plane looks like it will be late.

Finally, adequate and meaningful training should be provided to enhance
each KPI benchmark and supporting benchmarks. With proper training on
empowerment, decision-making, identifying critical success factors, and
how to create KPIs, there is a much greater chance of performance
measurement success.

For example, if an employee is empowered to stop a production line, he
or she must be adequately trained to know when it is viable to do so.

To use another example, don't require your employees to file unnecessary
reports. Many required monthly reports are rarely, if ever, read by the
recipients. As a result, they are a waste of time and resources.

## Know Your Critical Success Factors

Critical success factors (CSFs) should always link daily activities to
the organization's strategies. They have an ongoing impact on the
organization, and they are the source of all KPIs---the performance
measures that matter most.

To use the earlier example of the airlines, the KPI of daily monitoring
of plane departure and arrival times comes from the CSF of timeliness.

While certain strategies are the responsibility of a select group of
senior executives, according to the author, critical success factors
should be the focus of all levels of staff. They are intended to
influence and drive all "business as usual" strategic initiatives.

For example, the critical success factor of "delivery in full on time to
key customers" helps staff understand they need to service key customers
first, rather than fill orders that are the least complex first.

### Characteristics of CSFs

The author asserts that if you're unable to meaningfully explain to
staff what needs to be done well on a daily basis, you lack a clear
understanding of your company's CSFs.

Key characteristics of CSFs:

-   They must be understandable by all and promote buy-in to what is
    important to the organization.
-   They will reflect what top leadership already has discussed as
    success factors for the organization.
-   They are relevant across the organization.
-   They are limited to a manageable number, from five to eight.
-   They help point to other success factors and demonstrate their
    value.
-   They reflect a precise operational activity as opposed to being
    abstract statements.

CSFs should follow the SMART criteria attributed to Peter Drucker: they
are **specific** (avoiding empty and vague words), **measurable**
("timely departure and arrival of planes 24/7" is clearly measurable),
**achievable** (they are realistic), **relevant** (across the
organization), and **time-sensitive** (focused on the ongoing present,
not the past).

## Identify Your Winning KPIs

The author asserts that, contrary to popular belief, the primary driver
of employees is not money; rather, employees are more often driven by
respect, recognition, and self-actualization.

When financial incentives are tied to performance, they create key
**political indicators**, not key performance indicators. In other
words, employees will maneuver their success by who they know and how to
manipulate information rather than what they can do successfully. For
example, they might try to manipulate sales figures to get their
"bonus." These types of incentives are rarely linked to progress within
the organization's CSFs.

KPIs, on the other hand, align staff to the organization's critical
success factors and provide an ongoing indication of how teams are
performing in a way that is meaningful and drives true success.

## Avoid the Performance-Target Myth

Setting annual targets is another performance success myth. Such targets
are nearly impossible to meet---either they are too ambitious or they
stifle innovation and creativity.

The more effective approach is to have a moving target: regular
assessments of progress will indicate how much harder a team needs to
work. Conditions are always changing. For instance, even if you have
increased sales, those sales might not have kept up with the marketplace
growth rate. In other words, success is relative---not absolute.

KPIs are not financial. Rather, financial measures are result
indicators: they represent the activities that drive dollars. They
measure quantity, not quality or substance. It's important to have them,
but they should never take the place of KPIs, which are always tied to
CSFs.

## Make KPIs Mission Critical

The purpose of performance measurement is not to monitor strategic
initiatives, but rather to help the workforce focus on the critical
success factors of the organization.

For this to work, first you must identify your organization's mission,
vision, and values, and then achieve buy-in. Be prepared to abandon any
performance measures that don't support your mission, vision, and
values.

### Know Your Mission, Vision, and Values

Your organization's **mission statement** defines its reason for being.
It states the core purpose of the organization, and it should always be
customer-focused.

The **vision** should reflect what the organization should look like,
perhaps by outlining a specific action or goal, within a certain
timeframe. It is therefore more specific than a mission statement.

The **values** reflect what your organization stands for, often
following the format of "we believe..." Values might include qualities
such as respect, service excellence, and integrity. If your organization
has a strong mission, vision, and values, these can provide guideposts
for your KPI methodology.

## Have an Abandonment Strategy

Knowing what practices to give up is just as important to your KPI
process as identifying your critical success factors. The author
suggests these practices as part of your abandonment strategy:

-   Designate one day per month for teams to report what practices and
    projects no longer add value or necessity.
-   Measure your abandonment rate and report it to the CEO.
-   Be prepared to abandon all performance measures that are not aligned
    with your critical success factors. You might have to be willing to
    start the measurement practice from scratch.
-   Abandon gratuitous reporting practices. Have a clear purpose for
    each report, particularly the regularly occurring ones. Make sure
    they serve a valuable purpose.
-   Abandon the practice of performance-related pay linked to annual
    targets.
-   Abandon annual or biannual performance reviews and instead provide
    regular feedback---at least monthly.

## Develop a Reporting Framework

As the final practice in implementing an effective KPI program, your
teams will need to develop a reporting framework. Reporting your KPIs
will enable you to see their effectiveness as performance measures, and
will help you to see where changes need to be made or practices
abandoned.

In order to use reporting effectively, you will need to provide your
project team with appropriate training on how to report its performance
measures, and how to do so efficiently. KPI reporting, if done
instantaneously, will become routine---and will therefore be more
effective.

Key components of meaningful KPI reporting include:

-   being consistent;
-   including a component of trend analysis;
-   identifying an acceptable range for each measure;
-   ensuring the measures are simple and easy to understand;
-   ensuring they are easy to update;
-   ensuring they are accessible and not monetarily related;
-   including key turning points;
-   including a meaningful title.

## Insights --- Make KPIs Mission Critical

### Ensure Your KPIs Clearly Communicate Expectations

Make sure your KPIs clearly and simply communicate expectations.

For example, once your staff is clear that your KPI relates to the
timeliness of order fulfillment to key customers, they can focus their
energy on filling those orders first rather than the smaller orders.

### Don't Make KPIs Part of a Reward Structure

When financial incentives are tied to performance, they create key
political indicators, not key performance indicators. In other words,
employees will maneuver their success based on who they know rather than
what they do.

Make your KPI targets qualitative rather than quantitative.

### Monitor Current Activity to Determine Future Actions

Develop a robust reporting framework to monitor your KPI benchmarks.

Be prepared to modify your critical success factors if your KPIs are not
clearly defined and easy to measure. If these factors change, your
actions must change too.

### Be Ruthless about Abandoning Non-Delivering Processes

Be prepared to abandon all performance measures that don't reflect your
critical success factors or have an impact on daily operations.

You might have to be willing to reboot your measurement plan from the
ground up. For example, if your measurement criteria are vague, your
performance indicators will be too.

### Agree Upon a Definition of Successful KPIs

Make sure you have full organizational buy-in of your KPI definition.

For instance, if your KPI measures timeliness of flight arrivals, make
sure all employees agree on how timeliness is measured. Is it measured
by number of late arrivals or by how many minutes late a plane arrives?
Be as specific as possible.

## Conclusion

Developing strong key performance indicators is critical to your
organization's success and your employees' engagement.

David Parmenter explains how to create a winning KPI process that is
integral to your day-to-day operations and is valued by all, rather than
a top-down process.

If everyone knows what success looks like, success is more likely to be
attainable and sustainable.

> critical and will be achieved only if employees view performance
> measure information as valuable, useful, and worthwhile."

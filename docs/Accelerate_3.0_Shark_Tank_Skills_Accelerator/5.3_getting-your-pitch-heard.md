# Getting Your Pitch Heard

## Course Overview

Innovative ideas create business value only when they can be
communicated convincingly to the people who can support, fund, approve,
or implement them. This course provides a practical framework for
developing and delivering a business pitch: shape the idea, test whether
it is worth pursuing, understand the audience, construct a persuasive
argument, and deliver it effectively.

## 1. Getting Your Pitch Heard

Before building a pitch, clarify the idea and the business problem or
opportunity it addresses.

A strong pitch begins with an idea that can be transformed into a
compelling business proposition. The idea should be understandable,
relevant to the organization, and explainable in terms of concrete
benefits.

### Key questions

-   What problem or opportunity does the idea address?
-   Why does it matter now?
-   Who benefits?
-   What business value could it create?
-   What needs to happen for the idea to become reality?

The purpose of a pitch is not merely to describe an idea. It is to make
the audience understand why the idea deserves attention and action.

## 2. Evaluate Your Ideas

Before investing time in a full pitch, evaluate whether the idea is
genuinely worth pursuing.

### Characteristics of a good idea

A strong business idea is typically:

-   Interesting and meaningfully different.
-   Implementable with available resources and capabilities.
-   Supported by facts or evidence.
-   Pragmatic and relevant to organizational needs.
-   Capable of meeting a real requirement or objective.

Novelty alone is insufficient. An imaginative idea can still fail if it
requires unavailable resources, does not solve a meaningful problem, or
cannot realistically be executed.

### Practical evaluation

Assess:

-   Organizational fit
-   Implementation cost
-   Required skills and resources
-   Expected benefits
-   Risks and dependencies
-   Evidence of demand or usefulness
-   Similar previous initiatives

A useful idea should pass both an attractiveness test and a feasibility
test.

## 3. Why Pitches Fail

Even promising ideas can fail during the pitching process.

### Common causes

#### Poor organizational fit

An idea may be good in isolation but inappropriate for current
priorities, capabilities, resources, or circumstances.

#### Required resources are unavailable

Implementation may depend on funding, people, technology, time, or
specialist skills that cannot be provided.

#### Insufficient evidence

Decision-makers may hesitate to support a proposal based mainly on
intuition. Facts, research, customer feedback, precedents, and
comparable examples strengthen credibility.

#### The proposal has not been fully thought through

Stakeholders may identify operational, financial, technical, or
organizational issues that the presenter has overlooked.

#### Failure to learn from previous attempts

Earlier proposals and decisions can reveal recurring objections,
constraints, and organizational preferences.

The central lesson is that a clever idea does not sell itself. Identify
likely reasons for rejection before the pitch and address them.

## 4. Researching Your Audience

A pitch should be designed around the people who must support it.

Identify who has the authority, influence, expertise, or resources
necessary to move the idea forward.

### Understand your audience

Research:

-   Decision-makers and influencers
-   Their responsibilities and priorities
-   Previous decisions and preferences
-   Preferred communication styles
-   Evidence they tend to trust
-   Likely concerns and objections

Different audiences may value different forms of evidence, including
financial analysis, operational evidence, customer evidence, technical
feasibility, precedent, demonstrations, or quantitative data.

### Choose the right setting

Possible settings include:

-   Scheduled one-to-one meetings
-   Group presentations
-   Conferences
-   Informal conversations
-   Short elevator-style opportunities

Choose a context in which the audience can give the idea appropriate
attention.

## 5. Crafting the Perfect Pitch

A convincing pitch combines logic, relevance, evidence, adaptability,
and enthusiasm.

### Core structure

1.  **Problem or opportunity** --- establish why attention is required.
2.  **Proposed idea** --- explain the solution clearly.
3.  **Value** --- describe the benefits and expected impact.
4.  **Evidence** --- demonstrate why the idea is credible.
5.  **Feasibility** --- explain how it could realistically be
    implemented.
6.  **Ask** --- state the decision, support, funding, or next step
    required.

### Make the pitch adaptable

Prepare multiple versions:

-   A very short version for unexpected opportunities
-   A concise version for brief meetings
-   A full version for formal presentations and detailed discussion

### Support the argument

Useful material includes:

-   Facts and data
-   Customer or user evidence
-   Examples and precedents
-   Demonstrations or prototypes
-   Cost-benefit reasoning
-   Relevant anecdotes

Anecdotes can make the problem and solution concrete, but they should
reinforce rather than replace evidence.

### Deliver concisely

Do not overload the audience. Communicate the essential argument clearly
and leave room for questions and discussion.

## 6. Pitching in a Meeting

Group settings introduce interruptions, competing ideas, limited time,
different interests, and unpredictable questions.

### Useful techniques

-   Ask focused questions.
-   Respond directly to objections.
-   Connect comments back to the core proposal.
-   Adapt the level of detail to the room.
-   Use a concise pitch when attention is limited.
-   Keep the discussion focused on the target.

### Know your material

Be able to explain:

-   The opportunity or problem
-   The proposed approach
-   Expected benefits
-   Implementation requirements
-   Relevant risks
-   Supporting business rationale

Slides should support the presenter rather than substitute for
understanding.

### Presentation habits

Pay attention to:

-   Confidence
-   Voice and tone
-   Posture and body language
-   Clarity and brevity
-   Listening
-   Responsiveness to questions

Practice reveals unclear explanations, weak transitions, excessive
detail, unanswered objections, and timing problems.

## 7. Knowledge Check: Core Lessons

### What makes an idea suitable for a proposal?

A suitable idea should be:

-   Interesting or meaningfully different
-   Realistically implementable
-   Compatible with available skills and resources
-   Backed by evidence
-   Pragmatic
-   Aligned with organizational requirements

An idea does not have to be completely unprecedented. Novelty matters
only when combined with feasibility and relevance.

### What barriers can derail a pitch?

#### Contextual barriers

Organizational politics, priorities, constraints, or environmental
factors can prevent implementation.

#### Personal barriers

The presenter's reputation or history can influence reception. A history
of poorly developed ideas may make decision-makers less receptive.

#### Weaknesses in the idea

A proposal may fail to meet organizational requirements or exceed
available resources and capabilities.

### What should you know about the audience?

Before pitching, determine:

-   What evidence they trust
-   What types of ideas they prefer
-   When they are most receptive
-   Which communication style works best

Personal information unrelated to the decision is generally irrelevant.

### Essential elements of a convincing pitch

A strong pitch should be:

-   Created for the mindset of the target audience
-   Malleable and versatile
-   Well-reasoned and sound
-   Supported by a relevant anecdote when useful
-   Delivered concisely
-   Delivered with enthusiasm

Length and information density are not measures of quality.

### Effective meeting techniques

Useful techniques include:

-   Keeping the discussion on target
-   Monitoring and improving presentation habits
-   Selecting the best pitch format for the situation

Examples include confidently asking a relevant question, connecting
comments back to the proposal, and using a 30-second pitch when only a
brief window is available.

## Practical Pitch Framework

### Step 1 --- Define the idea

> We propose **\[solution\]** to address **\[problem/opportunity\]**,
> producing **\[business value\]**.

### Step 2 --- Test the idea

Ask:

-   Is it useful?
-   Is it feasible?
-   Does it align with organizational priorities?
-   Are the necessary resources available?
-   What evidence supports it?
-   What could prevent implementation?

### Step 3 --- Map the audience

Identify:

-   Decision-makers
-   Influencers
-   Subject-matter experts
-   Potential supporters
-   Likely opponents

Determine what matters most to each stakeholder.

### Step 4 --- Build the argument

**Problem → Idea → Evidence → Benefits → Feasibility → Ask**

### Step 5 --- Anticipate objections

Prepare for questions about:

-   Cost
-   Resources
-   Risk
-   Timing
-   Technical feasibility
-   Organizational fit
-   Competing priorities
-   Previous failed attempts

### Step 6 --- Prepare multiple versions

Create:

-   A **30-second pitch**
-   A **2--5 minute pitch**
-   A **full presentation**

All versions should communicate the same central argument at different
levels of detail.

### Step 7 --- Practice and refine

Ask reviewers:

-   What was unclear?
-   What was unconvincing?
-   What questions remained?
-   What seemed unnecessary?
-   What was the strongest part of the argument?

## 30-Second Pitch Template

> **\[Problem/opportunity\]** is currently causing **\[impact\]**. I
> propose **\[idea\]**, which would **\[primary benefit\]**. We have
> evidence from **\[evidence/source\]** suggesting that this is feasible
> and could deliver **\[expected value\]**. I would like **\[specific
> decision/action\]** so that we can **\[next step\]**.

## Key Takeaways

1.  A good pitch starts with a good idea, but a good idea alone is
    insufficient.
2.  Evaluate feasibility before trying to persuade others.
3.  Understand why similar ideas succeed or fail inside the
    organization.
4.  Research the audience and tailor the evidence, language, and
    setting.
5.  Structure the argument around a problem, solution, value, evidence,
    feasibility, and a clear ask.
6.  Prepare different versions for different time constraints.
7.  Anticipate objections before stakeholders expose weaknesses.
8.  Stay focused while remaining adaptable to questions and
    interruptions.
9.  Confidence, clarity, brevity, listening, and enthusiasm strengthen
    delivery.
10. Practice turns a good proposal into a more credible and persuasive
    pitch.

## One-Sentence Summary

> A successful business pitch combines a feasible, well-supported idea
> with deep audience understanding, a concise and adaptable argument,
> credible evidence, and confident delivery.
